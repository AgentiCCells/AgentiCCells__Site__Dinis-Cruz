A CV told as a story

A CV is usually a list of titles and dates. Dinis Cruz published his as a slide deck, and later asked people applying to his teams for a presentation about themselves. This page describes his deck, sums up his reasons, and gives you an outline to make your own.

His argument

This is our summary of his essay Using Presentations Instead of CVs in Hiring (8 June 2025). Read the essay for his own words.

  • A CV is a record, not a story. It lists past titles, dates and skills. It says little about how or why someone did the work, or what they could do next.
  • Slides are easier to take in. A reader can get the main points of a deck quickly.
  • The deck is itself a work sample. How someone chooses, orders and explains their own story shows how they think and communicate.
  • It helps people with unusual paths. A presentation lets someone explain a career change or a gap that a CV filter would reject.
  • Content matters more than polish. His teams valued a plain slide with real content over a designed slide with buzzwords.

How he used it in hiring

  • At Glasswall, where he was CTO and CISO, internship candidates from 2020 were asked to "do a presentation about yourself", alongside other tasks. The essay tells the story of a doctor moving into security who was hired as a security intern after a 10-slide deck about their learning plan and home lab. (essay)
  • At Holland & Barrett, where he was CISO, job adverts for his team asked for a presentation as well as a CV. HR agreed to it. The team gave candidates guidance on what to include and made a short video explaining why they ask. (essay, video on YouTube)
  • He was open about the costs. Not everyone is good at presentations, and a deck takes more time than a CV. His teams answered this with guidance, by asking for the deck later in the process, and sometimes by making it optional. (essay)

He describes the process as one that "makes them better": candidates learn something about their own strengths while making the deck, whatever the outcome. (a LinkedIn post of his, quoted in the essay)

Make your own

Team advice The outline below is advice from the AgentiCCells team, not Dinis's own. The second line on each slide is an illustration by the AgentiCCells team, built from Dinis's public record. It is not his deck. Each fact links to where we read it.

Ten slides is enough. Keep one idea on each. Write the deck for someone who has five minutes and has never met you.

Slide 1 of 10

  1. Slide 1: Who you are, in one line

    What goes on it: your name and a single line that says what you do now, in words a stranger would understand.

    What goes on it: your current work, and why it matters to the people you work for.

  3. Slide 3: The thread through your career

    What goes on it: the one theme that connects your jobs, so the reader sees a path and not a list.

    Illustration

    From Dinis's record: his work joins security and software development, and now generative AI. (diniscruz.ai/about)

  4. Slide 4: The roles that shaped you

    What goes on it: three or four roles, each with what you were there to change. Leave the dates to your LinkedIn profile.

Tips from the team

Team advice

  • Write the headings first. If the ten headings tell your story on their own, the deck works.
  • Use your own words. A deck that sounds like a template tells the reader nothing about you.
  • Show one real thing: a diagram, a screenshot, a page you wrote.
  • Give it a version number and a date, as Dinis did. A deck is easy to update; keep it current.
  • Publish it where people can find it, and link to it from your CV and your profile.
